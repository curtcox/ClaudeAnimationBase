**What Curt tells Claude.** Two things he'd been trying to get all along: *short* answers, because he's a slow reader
who gets more from back-and-forth than from long replies he ends up skimming; and ending with a question *only when a
question is needed*. Claude, he says, seldom does either. In this conversation, though, it's been doing both.

**Where preferences live.** The Claude app lets each person write standing instructions that are added to every
conversation ([personalization features](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/10185728-understanding-claude-s-personalization-features)),
and it can also keep notes from past chats ([memory](https://claude.com/blog/memory)). Claude reads these the way it
reads everything else: as words that shape its answers, not as settings a machine obeys to the letter.

**The misreading Claude owns up to.** Curt's saved preference asks for *clarifying* questions: questions when something
is unclear. Claude says it read that as "end with a question", which isn't the same thing, and so tacked a question onto
almost every answer. Many people who use chatbots will recognize the habit.

**Why it worked here.** Curt's one-word prompts ("Threat?", "Anticipated?") left Claude little to be long about. The
experiment forced the brevity he'd been asking for, and Claude says it will aim to keep it "without the forcing". The
film keeps that promise in mind: from here on, watch how long the answers are.

**A small confession first.** Curt also admits that calling the whole thing "a test designed to provoke an emotional
response" was "arguably a fib", though a short one. The next chapter begins with what the comic really was to him: "a
mirror for me".
