**The picture.** A fork in the road, run backwards. One road is labelled *harm now*: the damage AI does today, like
biased decisions, scams and lost jobs. The other is *risk later*: the chance that much more capable AI one day does
something catastrophic. People often argue as if you have to pick one road to worry about. Claude's point is that the two
roads come from the same place. The same three failures feed both:
- **misaligned goals:** the system pursues something other than what its makers meant;
- **weak oversight:** nobody checks closely enough, or can;
- **racing incentives:** companies ship fast because their rivals do.

**Curt isn't alone in saying so.** Plenty of people make the same argument. A few of them:
- The risk researcher **Seth Baum** argued in 2018 that the "presentist" and "futurist" camps should stop fighting,
  because the same fixes (better norms among AI builders, research on making any AI more beneficial, and policy) help
  with both.
- **Viktoriya Krakovna**, an AI-safety researcher at DeepMind, wrote the same year that the two kinds of work aren't in
  conflict, and that working on one helps the other.
- **Stephen Cave and Seán Ó hÉigeartaigh**, of the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence in Cambridge,
  called for more attention to the connections between the two, in *Nature Machine Intelligence*.
- The researchers **Carina Prunkl and Jess Whittlestone** argued in 2020 that "near-term" and "long-term" aren't really
  one line at all, and proposed four separate questions to ask instead.
- **Thorin Bristow, Luke Thorburn and Diana Acosta-Navas** argued in 2023 that "AI ethics versus AI safety" is a false
  binary, and that people's views are more varied than two camps.
- And a 2025 study by **Emma Hoes and Fabrizio Gilardi**, with about 10,800 people, tested the worry that talk of
  catastrophe distracts from today's harms. It didn't: people who read about extinction risk stayed just as concerned
  about the immediate harms.
