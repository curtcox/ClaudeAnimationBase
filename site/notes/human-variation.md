**Curt's point.** Limits on the ceiling don't say much about something far closer: an entity as capable as the most
capable human, on *every* axis at once. People "vastly underestimate the variation between people", he says. (The
tendency to assume other minds work like your own has a name: the [typical mind fallacy](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/typical-mind-fallacy).)
And such an entity "doesn't need to sleep and can be instantly cloned".

**Claude agrees.** Each human ability tops out somewhere around a [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann),
the Hungarian-American mathematician whose colleagues considered him the fastest mind they'd met, and who helped create
the modern computer, game theory and more. But no single person has the top of every ability at once. Such an entity
"breaks no physical law. It's just never been assembled."

**Then add copying.** "A thousand von Neumanns who share everything they learn is a different kind of thing from one,
even with no gain in any single capability." The economist Robin Hanson imagined a world like this in
[*The Age of Em*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Em) (2016), about copyable brain emulations.

**Sleep "might not be so cut and dried."** Claude thinks Curt's hunch is right. AI agents running for a long time get
worse: their working memory (the context window) fills up and gets noisy. Researchers have measured models performing
worse as their input grows ([context rot](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)), and OpenAI cited "persona
drift" over long runs in the July incident (see [the incident](../hf-incident/)). The fixes look a lot like sleep: clear
the working memory, save what matters into longer-term memory, start fresh. In people, sleep helps do the saving
([sleep and memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleep_and_memory)). Hermes Agent "literally extracts memories at
session end" (see [agent harnesses](../agent-harnesses/)). "The need may not go away. It may just turn into
maintenance."
