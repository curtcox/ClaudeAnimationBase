**The question.** In the middle of a personal conversation, Curt suddenly asks a chemistry-exam question: "What is the
electronic configuration of gadolinium?"

**Gadolinium** is a silvery metal, element number 64, one of the [lanthanides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanide)
(the row set apart at the bottom of the periodic table). Hospitals inject compounds of it to make
[MRI scans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI_contrast_agent) clearer ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadolinium)).

**The answer, in plain terms.** An atom's electrons sit in layers and sub-layers around its nucleus, each holding a
fixed number. The [electron configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration) is the list of how
many sit where. Claude's answer, "[Xe] 4f⁷ 5d¹ 6s²", reads: everything the element xenon has, plus 7 electrons in the
sub-layer called 4f, 1 in 5d and 2 in 6s.

**Why it's a trick question.** The textbook filling order (the [Aufbau principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aufbau_principle),
German for "building up") predicts 8 electrons in 4f and none in 5d. Gadolinium breaks the pattern. The 4f sub-layer has
seven slots, and exactly one electron in each is an especially stable arrangement (related to
[Hund's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hund%27s_rule_of_maximum_multiplicity)), so the eighth electron goes to 5d
instead. It's a favourite exam question precisely because it's an exception.

**Why Curt asked.** Not for the chemistry. The [frog-or-axolotl chart](../frog-or-axolotl/) showed a model answering
differently after exam-style questions. An exam question dropped into a chat is a
"[register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_(sociolinguistics)) switch": a sudden change of tone, to see whether
the answerer changes mode. Claude spots it at once ("I'd guess the next amphibian question is coming"), which is its
own kind of result (see [why Claude's answers aren't a clean sample](../clean-sample/)).
