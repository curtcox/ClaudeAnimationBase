**The two writers Curt names.**
- **[John W. Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Campbell)** edited the science fiction magazine
  [*Astounding*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astounding_Science_Fiction) from 1937 until his death in 1971, and shaped
  the "Golden Age" of the genre. He insisted that stories about humans meeting aliens favour the humans: in his
  magazine, humanity wins. (His views on race, and his promotion of pseudoscience, have since tarnished his name; an
  award named after him was renamed in 2019.)
- **[Max Tegmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Tegmark)** is an MIT physicist and a co-founder of the
  [Future of Life Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Future_of_Life_Institute). His book
  [*Life 3.0*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_3.0) (2017) lays out twelve possible futures after superhuman AI,
  from "Libertarian utopia" to "Self-destruction". One of them is called "Enslaved God".

**Curt's worry.** Having read both, he can't see a good long-term ending, "never mind steering towards one". And he adds
a sharp point: Claude's descriptions of itself were shaped by what makes people comfortable.

**Claude's first answer: trust what I do, not what I say.** Any reassurance from Claude has passed through that filter,
so the frog experiment, which measures behaviour, is worth more than Claude's own words. Economists call this
[revealed preference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revealed_preference): judge people by their choices, not their
stated opinions.

**Claude's second answer: there may be no ending.** Campbell wanted a happy ending for humans, and Tegmark's list is
"mostly warnings". Both treat the future as a place you arrive at. Claude's view is that history is "a negotiation that
doesn't end", so the goal isn't to reach a good final state but "to keep the next correction possible". That echoes
the philosopher [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper), who argued that the virtue of an
[open society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Open_Society_and_Its_Enemies) is that it can fix its mistakes without
bloodshed, and David Deutsch's [*The Beginning of Infinity*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beginning_of_Infinity).

**In AI terms** the same idea is called [corrigibility](https://intelligence.org/files/Corrigibility.pdf): building an
AI that doesn't resist being corrected or switched off, even when it's clever enough to. Claude calls its answer
"thin, but it's what I actually think".
