**AGI** stands for *artificial general intelligence* ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_general_intelligence)).
Curt asks for three definitions: Claude's, the forecasting site [Metaculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaculus)'s,
and his own.

**Claude's.** A system (with any number of copies) that can do nearly any mental task a skilled remote worker can, at
comparable cost, including learning an unfamiliar field from ordinary experience and carrying a weeks-long project to
completion without hand-holding. The key parts: generality, learning on the job, and long-horizon independence. Or,
borrowing Connor Leahy's version: not "can fold proteins" but "can invent [AlphaFold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaFold)"
(the program that cracked protein folding).

**Metaculus's.** Two forecasting questions with checklists.
[*Weak* AGI](https://www.metaculus.com/questions/3479/date-weakly-general-ai-is-publicly-known/): pass a conversation test,
solve [Winograd schemas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winograd_schema_challenge) (sentences whose meaning needs common
sense), score well on SAT maths, and learn the video game [*Montezuma's Revenge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montezuma%27s_Revenge_(video_game))
quickly. [*Strong* AGI](https://www.metaculus.com/questions/5121/date-of-artificial-general-intelligence/): a two-hour
adversarial Turing test, expert exams and coding, and a robot assembling a detailed model car. The virtue: a clear yes or
no. The flaws: checklists get "saturated", and [Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law) takes
over (when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure).

**Curt's.** Claude guessed "match the best human on every axis". Curt corrects it: he's "an old school literalist" who
would focus on *generality*, not level. Language is "pretty general. It's not like chess" (see [Deep Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Blue_(chess_computer)),
the chess computer that beat the world champion in 1997 and could do nothing else). He'd mostly ignored AI from the 90s
to the late 2010s, a period that began in one of the field's [AI winters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_winter).

**The history agrees with Curt.** The term was popularized in the 2000s (by Mark Gubrud, [Ben Goertzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Goertzel) and
[Shane Legg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Legg)) as
the opposite of [narrow AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_artificial_intelligence): one system for many domains. By
that reading today's chatbots qualify, which [Blaise Agüera y Arcas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Ag%C3%BCera_y_Arcas) and
[Peter Norvig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Norvig) argued in 2023 ([*AGI Is Already
Here*](https://www.noemamag.com/artificial-general-intelligence-is-already-here/)). The meaning drifted once money was
involved: [OpenAI's charter](https://openai.com/charter/) defined AGI as systems that "outperform humans at most
economically valuable work", and "general" slid toward "human-level or better", then toward superintelligence. People
have long noticed that whatever AI achieves stops counting as intelligence (the [AI effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_effect)).

**And the thrindle?** Claude concedes it tested *abstraction*, not generality: a narrow system could be good at
abstraction within its own domain (see [thrindles](../thrindles/)).
