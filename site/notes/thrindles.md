**The idea behind the test.** Knowing a word isn't having a concept. The tell is whether you can apply an idea to cases
you've never seen, including tricky ones, and explain why. So Claude invented a word nobody could have memorized.

**A thrindle** is "anything that gets less useful the more people use it at once": a highway at rush hour, a shared
Wi-Fi network. (Economists have a word for things one person's use takes from another's:
[rival goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivalry_(economics)). A thrindle is roughly a crowdable one.) Claude asked:
is a language a thrindle? A lighthouse? A secret?

**Curt's answer**, and what Claude read in it:
- **"Navajo is a language and a secret."** A real insight. In the Second World War, the US Marines used
  [Navajo code talkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_talker): the [Navajo language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_language)
  was so little known outside the Navajo Nation that it served as an unbreakable code. As a code, every extra speaker
  (especially an enemy one) made it less useful: a perfect thrindle. As an ordinary language, it's the opposite: more
  speakers make it more useful, which economists call a [network effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_effect).
- **"A lighthouse must be a thrindle."** A near miss. Crowded water is the thrindle; the light shines as well for a
  hundred ships as for one. The lighthouse is economists' favourite example of a [public good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_good_(economics)),
  one person's use takes nothing from another's (a famous paper argues about exactly this: [*The Lighthouse in Economics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lighthouse_in_Economics)).
- **"Since languages can be secrets…, a lighthouse must be a thrindle."** A deliberate joke. You can only break a rule of
  logic on purpose if you have the rule.

**The second probe, and Curt's better move.** Asked for something that's a thrindle for some people and the opposite for
others, Curt noticed a hidden flaw in the definition: "Useful is only useful to someone." Usefulness isn't a property of
a thing on its own but a relation between the thing and its user (philosophers distinguish
[intrinsic and extrinsic properties](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intrinsic-extrinsic/)). Then an example: the
[cliché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clich%C3%A9). The more people use one, the better it works as shared shorthand,
and the more a writer (or an English teacher) hates it.

**The verdict, and the catch.** Curt has concepts. But Claude would probably pass the same probes, so the test can't
separate the cases. That's the problem for anyone claiming AI has no concepts (see
[the case against AI concepts](../concepts-case/)).
